## SOURCE_SCOPE

This cutpack covers the full text of Weis (2013). The book is organized around:
- **Structural framing**: support/resistance lines, trend lines, channels, reverse channels, axis lines, apexes.
- **Bar-by-bar logic**: sequential reading of price range, position of close, and volume across 10 abstract two-day patterns.
- **Action signals**: springs (false breakdowns), upthrusts (false breakouts), absorption, and secondary tests.
- **Tape reading adaptations**: intraday wave charts, volume-figure charts, and cumulative volume differences (Weis Wave) derived from Wyckoff’s original tape-reading tools.
- **Chart studies**: step-by-step walkthroughs of U.S. Steel, Louisiana Pacific, Arch Coal, S&P, Dow, sterling/yen, silver, and others.
- **Point-and-Figure / Renko**: brief coverage as projection/compression tools, not primary signals.

**Single-book scope**: This cutpack does not merge with other Wyckoff sources, price-action primers, or footprint/DOM literature. Cross-references are kept to the minimum needed to explain the book’s own terminology.

## RETAINED_EXCERPTS

### RE-001: Where to Find Trades (Trading-Range Edges)

> “Finding trades is like finding fish. Fish can be randomly caught in any part of a lake, but they tend to congregate in specific areas at different times of the year. Similarly, big trades can be hooked at any point on a chart, but they appear with greater frequency around the edges of trading ranges.” (p. 25)

> “The boundaries of trading ranges are repeatedly tested and/or penetrated as the buyers and sellers struggle for dominance. Whenever the boundaries are breached, follow-through or the lack of follow-through becomes the deciding factor.” (p. 25)

> “After breakouts or breakdowns occur, prices often retest these areas.” (p. 25)

### RE-002: Drawing Lines — Support, Resistance, Axis

> “Drawing support and resistance lines might seem the subject of Charting 101. … Even fewer have learned to recognize horizontal lines around which prices have revolved.” (p. 29)

> “When a line alternately serves as support and resistance, I refer to it as an axis line. Prices tend to revolve around these axis lines.” (p. 45)

> “The support/resistance lines also frame the two trading ranges on this portion of the chart. By drawing the lines, we can better observe the attempts of either side to break out, as took place during the trench warfare of WWI. The numerous false breakouts on either side of a trading range are important tests of the opposing forces’ strength.” (p. 45)

### RE-003: Trend Lines, Channels, Reverse Channels

> “Trend lines depict the angle of advance or decline. They are dynamic support and resistance lines as opposed to the static horizontal lines that frame trading ranges.” (p. 33)

> “In a downtrend, a trend line is drawn across successively lower highs. … In an uptrend, a trend line is drawn across the rising supports. It is called the demand line as it marks the point where buying repeatedly emerges. Similarly, the downtrend line across highs is called the supply line.” (p. 33)

> “A rally above the top of an up-channel is often a better overbought indication than most mathematical tools.” (p. 37)

> “Some uptrends will not fit in the normal channels we have previously discussed. Because of their steepness, they require drawing a reverse trend line across rising highs—points 1 and 2. … When prices are falling within a pattern of converging lines, it usually signifies the decline is nearing a low.” (p. 39)

> “Exceeding up and down reverse trend lines must put one on alert for a trend reversal. No other trend line break has such predictive value.” (p. 42)

### RE-004: Apex (Contraction) and Confluence of Lines

> “By itself, an apex has little or no predictive value. It simply indicates the amplitude of price swings has narrowed to a point of equilibrium between the forces of supply and demand. This equilibrium cannot continue indefinitely; it will be shattered. One looks for price/volume clues that indicate the future direction.” (p. 49)

> “When there is such a confluence of lines, one should be alert to the possibility of a turning point. I rarely establish a trade based on the evidence of trend lines alone. … other factors are taken into account.” (p. 47)

> “Price tightness is the hallmark of an apex.” (p. 49)

### RE-005: Sequential Bar-Reading Logic (10 Two-Day Patterns)

> “When studying a bar chart, we go through a process of sequential evaluation. This normally consists of comparing the immediate price movement with the most recent price bars. From this comparison, we make deductions or inferences about what to expect in the next time period.” (p. 65)

> Key principles extracted from the 10 abstract patterns (pp. 66–71):
- Ease of movement (wide vs. narrow range) signals force.
- Position of the close within the range signals who won the session.
- Narrow range after a thrust = hinge / dead center = be alert for decisive move.
- Close near midrange after a down-thrust = buying present at lower levels.
- Key reversal: fall below previous low, then close above previous high = vacuum below, buyers stepped in.
- Gap + narrow range + close near low = no ability to rally, sellers still in control.
- Clustering of closes without ability to sustain a rally = temporary support, not strong.

> “The position of the close on day ‘a’ indicates buying appeared at the lower level of the range. It has a bullish connotation. Day ‘b’ shows a total lack of movement. In the Wyckoff lexicon, this kind of day is referred to as a ‘hinge’ from which a larger swing may occur.” (p. 50)

### RE-006: Volume and Effort vs. Reward

> “Volume is best interpreted in conjunction with the price range and the position of the close. … We compare the force (or effort) of the buying and selling against the reward (price gain or loss) to determine which side is dominant and to identify signs of an imminent change in trend.” (p. 53)

> “The market is like a slowly revolving wheel: Whether the wheel will continue to revolve in the same direction, stand still or reverse depends entirely upon the forces which come in contact with it hub and tread.” (p. 53)

> “Rallies and sell-offs on very heavy volume can indicate climactic or stopping action. Rallies and sell-offs on very low volume often signify exhaustion.” (p. 53)

> “Heavy volume and slight downward progress reflect little reward for the effort because demand is emerging at lower levels.” (p. 53)

### RE-007: Springs — Definition, Structure, Trigger, Invalidation

> “For me, a spring is a washout (penetration) of a trading range or support level that fails to follow through and leads to an upward reversal.” (p. 73)

> “The potential gain created by a spring on a five-minute or monthly chart is a function of the underlying trend, market volatility and point and figure preparation.” (p. 73)

> Three important concepts regarding springs:
> 1. “When a market breaks below a well-defined support line and fails to follow through, I consider it to be in a ‘potential spring position.’ … It does not guarantee a spring.” (p. 73)
> 2. “Springs in an uptrend have a higher percentage of success. Contrarily, if a potential spring in a downtrend fails to develop, short sellers gain useful trading information.” (p. 73)
> 3. “A market’s volatility often dictates the size of a spring’s upward reversal. The amount of preparation, that is, the size of the trading range, can also determine the magnitude of the up-move generated by the spring.” (p. 73)

> “A ‘relatively small penetration’ gives the right look. Such terminology does not convey much meaning; the meaning comes through experience.” (p. 77)

> “On the QQQ chart … the spring began on May 22 where the stock broke below 78 and reversed upward. At this point, the buyers appeared to overcome the heavy selling; however, the buying evaporated on May 23 and prices reversed downward to close below the trading range and near the day’s low. The boxer has been soundly knocked out of the ring and into the fifth row of seats. He is not supposed to recover. Yet, on May 24, the stock reverses upward again and closes on a strong note above the lower boundary of the trading range. The heavy volume and strong close indicate the force of the buying has overcome the force of the selling.” (p. 75)

> “Many springs—especially those where the breakdown occurred on heavy volume—are retested. The test of a spring offers an excellent opportunity to go long as it represents higher support. Ideally, the daily range should narrow and the volume taper off on the secondary test of a spring.” (p. 79)

> “I know of no better trading strategy than the spring. It produces short-term intraday trades and ignites many long-term trends. For risk management, it offers a way to enter a trade at the danger point where the outcome will be quickly determined and the risk is minimal.” (p. 94)

> **Failed springs in a downtrend**: “Downtrends are littered with failed springs (see Figure 5.6). They create what I call a bottom picker’s nightmare, as they repeatedly entice traders to chase upward reversals. These are usually short lived and, when read correctly, can be used for establishing short positions.” (p. 87)

### RE-008: Upthrusts — Definition, Structure, Trigger, Invalidation

> “When a stock, index, or commodity moves above a previous line of resistance and fails to follow through, one has to consider the potential for a downward reversal. Such a failed breakout is called an upthrust.” (p. 95)

> “I find upthrusts to be more difficult to trade than springs. One reason may be the public’s willingness to buy new highs. They are not as anxious to go short on new lows.” (p. 95)

> “Because of the potential for an upthrust, I am not eager to buy breakouts.” (p. 95)

> “The first prerequisite is a previous high across which a resistance line is drawn. A move above that line becomes a ‘potential’ upthrust. The rise above a previous high may not be the deciding price action. It’s often the cumulative behavior on the preceding price bars and especially the succeeding price bars that tell the story.” (p. 97)

> “A narrow-range breakout does not speak of aggressive demand capable of resuming an uptrend. But it’s the high-volume sell-offs in the next two sessions, April 11 and 12, that prove the sellers have taken control.” (p. 97)

> “No precise size measurement for an upthrust to fulfill. A new high by 10 to 15 percent seems a reasonable limitation for an upthrust.” (p. 99)

> “Supposed upthrusts in an uptrend rarely pan out. In a downtrend, however, upthrusts above a previous correction high have a greater probability of working. They flourish.” (p. 97)

> “In a top formation, there may be several upthrusts before the markdown begins.” (p. 105)

> “When a market continues to hold against a resistance level and refuses to turn down after several instances of threatening price action, one has to consider absorption is taking place.” (p. 105)

### RE-009: Absorption — Definition and Clues

> “Absorption is the process through which the long liquidation, profit taking, and new short selling are overcome. It can show up on any chart, regardless of the time frame.” (p. 107)

> Clues pointing to successful absorption:
> - “Rising supports.” (p. 107)
> - “Volume increases around the top of the absorption area.” (p. 107)
> - “Lack of downward follow-through after a threatening price bar.” (p. 107)
> - “At the right-hand side of an absorption area, prices tend to press against the resistance line without giving ground.” (p. 107)
> - “In some instances, the absorption phase is resolved by a spring.” (p. 107)
> - “Minor up-thrusts during absorption fail to produce a breakdown.” (p. 107)

> “Absorption areas are generally shallow. They often form in the area where prices recently accelerated higher and/or where volume rose sharply.” (p. 108)

> “When the persistent selling against a low fails to produce further weakness, Wyckoff referred to such behavior as ‘bag-holding.’ In this situation, the large operators are trapping the shorts.” (p. 108)

> “Distinguishing between an upthrust and absorption sometimes requires paying attention to subtle clues. … This chart demonstrates why one cannot automatically consider going short when prices fail to immediately follow through after breakouts. It underscores why chart reading is an art rather than a science built on black and white signals.” (p. 111)

### RE-010: Shortening of the Thrust (SOT)

> “Wyckoff referred to this behavior as shortening of the thrust. (On charts, I often designate this behavior with the abbreviation SOT; it also occurs on up-moves.) Shortening of the thrust reflects loss of momentum.” (p. 81)

> “Whenever the volume becomes heavy at the low points of each down-move and the downward progress diminishes, pay close attention. It means the big effort earned little reward because demand is emerging at lower levels.” (p. 81)

> “The main idea behind the notion of springs, upthrusts, and shortening of the thrust is lack of follow-through. It goes to the heart of the matter.” (p. 81)

### RE-011: Ice Lines (Modern Wyckoff Metaphor)

> “In the modern-day Wyckoff course, the line of support across the bottom of a trading range is compared to the ice covering a frozen pond. It is called the ice line. Wyckoff never used this term, but it provides a memorable metaphor.” (p. 49)

> “The 1977–2000 downtrend on the cocoa yearly chart demonstrates price repeatedly interacting with previous support or ice lines. … In fact, support lines drawn across the bottom of any trading range—on a yearly or an hourly chart—can be viewed in this light.” (p. 49)

### RE-012: Tape Reading — Volume-Figure Charts and Wave Charts (Weis Wave)

> “Wyckoff knew tape reading cannot be reduced to a set of specific instructions. It’s like dancing. You can learn the basic dance steps, but in order to dance you must have the feel of the music.” (p. 140)

> “Tape interpretation depends upon consideration of the action of the volume … The action of the volume tells us of the supply and demand; price merely denotes the value of the volume.” (p. 128)

> “I modified the volume-figure chart into a wave chart with a 1/8-point scale and a 1/4-point wave or reversal. This filters out all of the 1/8-point reactions within a wave … and reduces the size of the chart.” (p. 135)

> “For a modern-day market, with thousands of intraday price changes, such a chart would be impractical. My final rendition … plot the volume as a histogram below the corresponding price movement.” (p. 141)

> “Remember when the selling waves begin to diminish in length and volume (duration) and the buying waves increase, the trend is reversing upward.” (p. 161)

> “When the duration, size, and volume of the selling waves begin to increase … one should be alert to a possible change of trend.” (p. 147)

> “The diminished volume on the second buying wave and the shortening of the upward thrust make us wary of a possible trend change.” (p. 147)

### RE-013: True Range as Volume Proxy

> “I have observed how a large true range often coincides with heavy volume. I have previously shown how true range can serve as a proxy for volume and especially for markets or yield indices where no volume data are available.” (p. 122)

> “In an uptrend, it encompasses the distance from the previous close to the current day’s high. Thus, the true range includes gaps. The true range in a downtrend spans the distance from the previous day’s close to the next bar’s low.” (p. 122)

### RE-014: Point-and-Figure Counts (Cautionary)

> “Point-and-figure counts are purely mechanical and have no magical powers. Sometimes they have pinpoint accuracy, and other times they fail miserably. No trade should be established solely because of point-and-figure counts. They represent potential.” (p. 131)

> “Congestion areas on point-and-figure charts theoretically depict the amount of cause or preparation that has been built for a potential move.” (p. 131)

## CORE_CONCEPTS

### CC-001: Trading-Range Edges as Primary Location
- **Rule**: Trades cluster around the edges of trading ranges (support/resistance boundaries), not in the middle.
- **Context constraint**: DO NOT chase trades in the middle of a wide trading range unless there is a clear axis line or confluence providing a local edge.
- **Constraint**: Range size matters. A 20% range on a daily stock chart is “intermediate size”; a sub-1% range on an hourly chart is small and may resolve quickly.
- **Failure mode**: Buying a breakout or selling a breakdown without waiting for follow-through (or lack thereof) confirmation leads to whipsaws.

### CC-002: Horizontal Lines (Support / Resistance / Axis)
- **Rule**: Draw horizontal lines across prior highs and prior lows that have halted price. Extend them forward. Watch where price interacts with them again.
- **Axis line**: A line that has served as both support and resistance at different times; prices revolve around it.
- **Scene constraint**: Axis lines alone do not signal to buy or sell. They frame the “danger point” where the next battle will occur. Action requires bar-by-bar confirmation (close, range, volume).
- **Failure mode**: Forcing a line to fit (drawing through price to reach an anchor) invalidates the line.

### CC-003: Trend Lines and Channels
- **Demand line (uptrend)**: Connects two rising lows. Parallel supply line drawn across an intervening high forms an up-channel.
- **Supply line (downtrend)**: Connects two falling highs. Parallel demand line drawn across an intervening low forms a down-channel.
- **Reverse trend line / reverse channel**: For steep trends, draw across rising highs (reverse up) or falling lows (reverse down). A break of a reverse trend line/channel often marks the end of a swing.
- **Converging lines (wedge)**: Normal trend line + reverse trend line converging = rally tiring or decline nearing a low. This is the only pattern Weis explicitly considers meaningful.
- **Overbought/oversold within channel**: A rally above the supply line of an up-channel or below the demand line of a down-channel = potential climax, not automatic reversal.

### CC-004: Sequential Bar Reading (The 10 Patterns and Beyond)
- **Core logic**: Compare day “a” to day “b” on range, position of close, and (later) volume. Infer what day “c” is likely to do, while accepting that news can gap the market.
- **Key reversal**: Close below prior low → close above prior high. Indicates vacuum below; buyers stepped in. Low of day “b” becomes stop for longs.
- **Hinge**: Very narrow range day with little movement = dead center. Alerts for a decisive move on the next session.
- **Ease of movement**: Wide-range bars = force; narrow-range bars = lack of force or exhaustion.
- **Clustering of closes**: Two or more closes at the same level without ability to rally away = temporary support, not strong reversal.

### CC-005: Springs (Failed Breakdowns / Washouts)
- **Definition**: Penetration below a well-defined support level or trading-range bottom, followed by failure to continue lower and an upward reversal.
- **Structural prerequisites**:
  1. A prior, well-defined support line (horizontal or demand line).
  2. A breakdown (close below or intraday thrust below) that produces little or no follow-through.
  3. Reversal bar with strong close and/or increased volume, confirming buyers overwhelmed sellers.
- **Volume profile**:
  - Heavy-volume breakdown + strong reversal = buyers absorbed all supply; high probability spring.
  - Low-volume breakdown + reversal = supply was spent; also valid, but may not retest.
- **Secondary test**: After a spring, price often returns to the broken level. Narrow range + low volume on the retest = higher-probability long entry. Aggressive entry = on the reversal day itself or on the next open with stop below the spring low.
- **Trend context**: Springs in uptrends have higher success rates. Springs in downtrends often fail and become “bottom picker’s nightmares.”
- **Invalidation**: Close below the spring low on follow-through = breakdown genuine, not a spring. Reversal below the low of the spring reversal day = spring failed.
- **Target logic**: Magnitude depends on size of the trading range (preparation), underlying trend, and volatility. No fixed R:R; partial profits on retest of prior range top, remainder with trailing stop.

### CC-006: Upthrusts (Failed Breakouts)
- **Definition**: Penetration above a well-defined resistance level or trading-range top, followed by failure to continue higher and a downward reversal.
- **Structural prerequisites**:
  1. A prior, well-defined resistance line (horizontal or supply line).
  2. Breakout above the line.
  3. Cumulative behavior on succeeding bars (not just the breakout bar) shows sellers regaining control: weak close, narrowing range, high volume on the reversal.
- **Narrow-range breakout**: A breakout with a narrow range is suspicious and should be eyed suspiciously. It does not speak of aggressive demand.
- **Trend context**: Upthrusts in uptrends rarely pan out; they tend to produce short-lived declines or lateral movement. Upthrusts in downtrends (rallies to prior correction highs) have greater probability of working.
- **Size guideline**: “A new high by 10 to 15 percent seems a reasonable limitation for an upthrust.” (p. 99). This is a rough heuristic, not a system parameter.
- **Invalidation**: Close above the upthrust high with expanding volume and follow-through = breakout genuine, not an upthrust. Multiple upthrusts can occur in a top before markdown begins; the first upthrust failure does not guarantee a major top.
- **Stop logic**: Short entry on close or next open after reversal confirmation; stop above the high of the upthrust day.

### CC-007: Absorption
- **Definition**: The process by which large operators absorb overhead supply (at highs) or bid support (at lows) so that the trend can continue.
- **Location**: Usually at the top of a trading range (after a breakout) or at the bottom (after a breakdown). Also occurs along a trend as “bag-holding” (trapping shorts at lows or trapping longs at highs).
- **Clues of absorption at highs**:
  - Rising supports during the consolidation.
  - Volume increases around the top of the absorption area.
  - Lack of downward follow-through after threatening bars.
  - Prices press against the resistance line without giving ground on the right-hand side.
  - Minor upthrusts during absorption fail to produce a breakdown.
- **Clues of absorption at lows (bag-holding)**:
  - Prices hug the low but cannot break down further.
  - Repeated springs that fail to develop = sellers absorbing buying.
- **Distinguishing from top/bottom**: Requires judgment. Absorption can look like a top or bottom; the difference is whether the next thrust breaks through with force or reverses. Weis explicitly warns this is “an art rather than a science.”
- **Scene constraint**: Do NOT short a failed breakout if the broader context is absorption in an uptrend. Do NOT buy a failed breakdown if the broader context is distribution.

### CC-008: Shortening of the Thrust (SOT)
- **Definition**: Successive down-waves (or up-waves) within a trend produce smaller and smaller price progress despite similar or increasing volume.
- **Interpretation**: Big effort, little reward = opposing force is emerging. Heavy volume at the low points with diminishing downward progress = demand emerging. Heavy volume at high points with diminishing upward progress = supply emerging.
- **Usage**: SOT is used to confirm that a spring or upthrust is developing within a larger trend context, not as a standalone signal.
- **Proxy**: On OHLCV bars, compare the point progress of each thrust (high-to-low or close-to-close) across a sequence of 3–5 bars.

### CC-009: Tape Reading / Wave Charts (Weis Wave)
- **Definition**: An adaptation of Wyckoff’s tape-reading method where intraday price changes are grouped into waves (swings) based on a minimum reversal size (e.g., 3/32nds for bonds). The volume of each wave is summed and plotted as a histogram.
- **Core logic**: Compare buying-wave volume, length, and duration vs. selling-wave volume, length, and duration. When selling waves diminish in length and volume while buying waves increase = trend reversing upward. The reverse = trend reversing downward.
- **Shortening of thrust on waves**: A buying wave that covers less ground with more (or equal) volume than the prior buying wave = demand tiring.
- **Context**: Used primarily for intraday timing and confirmation. Requires tick-level or sub-minute data and volume. Not suitable for end-of-day-only systems.
- **Scene constraint**: Wave-chart analysis is an “intuitive judgment” developed through practice. Cannot be reduced to a mechanical rule set.

## FORMULAS_AND_ALGOS

### FA-001: Minimal Proxy — Spring Detection (End-of-Day, OHLCV)

Inputs:
  lookback = 20 to 50 bars (default 20)
  swing_threshold = 1.5 × ATR(lookback)
  support_level = most recent swing_low(lookback)

Trigger (potential spring):
  1. Low(bar_t) < support_level
  2. Close(bar_t) > support_level  OR  Close(bar_t) > Close(bar_t-1)
  3. Range(bar_t) >= 0.5 × ATR(lookback)  (not a doji/nothing bar)

Confirmation (stronger spring):
  4. Volume(bar_t) > Volume(bar_t-1)  OR  Volume(bar_t) > SMA(Volume, 5)
  5. Close_position = (Close - Low) / (High - Low) > 0.6

Invalidation:
  Close(bar_t+1) < Low(bar_t)  →  breakdown genuine, spring failed.

Filter:
  Higher-timeframe trend = uptrend (e.g., Close > 20-period SMA on daily)
  →  higher probability.  Downtrend = lower probability / short-bias only.

- **Proxy/approximation label**: This is a minimal proxy. The book’s actual spring judgment involves axis-line context, prior volume structure, and “right look” that cannot be reduced to a single formula.
- **NOT a trading system**: No fixed R:R, no position sizing, no automated entry.

### FA-002: Minimal Proxy — Upthrust Detection (End-of-Day, OHLCV)

Inputs:
  lookback = 20 to 50 bars
  resistance_level = most recent swing_high(lookback)

Trigger (potential upthrust):
  1. High(bar_t) > resistance_level
  2. Close(bar_t) < resistance_level  OR  Close(bar_t) < Close(bar_t-1)
  3. Range(bar_t) >= 0.5 × ATR(lookback)

Confirmation (stronger upthrust):
  4. Volume(bar_t+1) > Volume(bar_t)  on a down-bar  (sellers follow through)
  5. Close(bar_t+1) < Low(bar_t)  OR  Close(bar_t+1) < Open(bar_t+1) with range > prior bar

Invalidation:
  Close(bar_t+1) > High(bar_t)  →  breakout genuine, upthrust failed.

Filter:
  Higher-timeframe trend = downtrend → higher probability.
  Uptrend → suspected upthrust often fails; treat as absorption warning instead.

- **Proxy/approximation label**: Same caveats as FA-001. The book emphasizes cumulative behavior across multiple bars, not a single bar flag.

### FA-003: Minimal Proxy — Shortening of the Thrust (SOT)

Inputs:
  thrust_count = 3 to 5 consecutive down-swings (or up-swings)
  thrust_measure = |Close_i - Close_{i-1}|  (or High_i - Low_i)

Detection:
  For each down-swing i (identified by lower low and lower high):
    thrust_i = |Close_i - Close_{i-1}|
  Fit linear regression to [thrust_1, thrust_2, ..., thrust_n].
  slope < 0  AND  mean_volume(last_n) >= mean_volume(prior_n)
  →  SOT alert.

Context requirement:
  Price must be near a known support level (spring context)
  or near a known resistance level (upthrust context).
  SOT alone is not a signal.

### FA-004: Minimal Proxy — Narrow-Range Contraction (Apex / Hinge)

Inputs:
  contraction_ratio = ATR(5) / ATR(20)
  threshold = 0.5

Alert:
  contraction_ratio < threshold  AND  range(bar_t) < 0.5 × ATR(20)
  →  apex/hinge alert.

Resolution:
  Wait for next bar to close above recent high or below recent low
  with range > 1.0 × ATR(20).
  →  direction resolved; size of move estimated from recent range width.

### FA-005: Minimal Proxy — Effort vs. Reward (Volume Proxy)

Inputs:
  effort = Volume(bar_t)
  reward = |Close(bar_t) - Open(bar_t)|  OR  TrueRange(bar_t)

Ratio:
  effort_reward = effort / reward

Interpretation (relative to 10-bar lookback):
  effort_reward > 1.5×median  AND  reward < 0.5×median
  →  large effort, small reward = opposing force likely present.

If volume unavailable (FX spot):
  Replace effort with TrueRange(bar_t) as proxy.

## NOT_QUANT_YET

### NQ-001: The “Right Look” of Penetration Size
- **Why**: Weis states “a ‘relatively small penetration’ gives the right look. Such terminology does not convey much meaning; the meaning comes through experience as when we eye a board and say it measures about one foot.” (p. 77)
- **Retention**: Keep as trading-language asset. No fixed proxy is valid across all markets, volatilities, and timeframes. A 1% penetration in EUR/USD may be large on a daily chart but tiny on a 5-minute chart.
- **Path forward**: A volatility-normalized penetration metric (e.g., penetration depth / ATR(20)) could be tested, but the book explicitly rejects this kind of precision.

### NQ-002: Chart Reading as Art / Intuition
- **Why**: Weis and Wyckoff emphasize “intuitive judgment” and “the feel of the music.” The book contains numerous warnings that chart reading is gray, not black and white.
- **Retention**: This is a methodological boundary condition. It belongs in the structured note as a warning against over-automation.
- **Path forward**: Can be retained as a rule-set sanity check (e.g., “if 3 out of 5 conditions are met, flag for review”), but not as a hard threshold.

### NQ-003: Tape Reading / Wave Chart Intuition
- **Why**: The wave-chart method (Weis Wave) requires summing volume across intraday swings defined by a subjective reversal threshold. The exact threshold (e.g., 3/32nds for bonds) is market-specific and must be adjusted by volatility.
- **Retention**: The concept of wave-volume comparison is valuable, but the exact implementation requires tick or sub-minute data and discretionary threshold selection.
- **Path forward**: Could be approximated with a “swing volume” indicator on 1-minute or 5-minute data using a fixed ATR-based reversal threshold, but this is a research project, not a ready field.

### NQ-004: Absorption vs. Distribution Discrimination
- **Why**: Weis repeatedly notes that absorption looks like a top and vice versa. The distinction depends on subtle clues (rising supports, volume clustering, failed upthrusts) that resist a single formula.
- **Retention**: Keep the checklist of clues (CC-007) as a structured note. Build a multi-factor proxy label in future research, but do not deploy as a live signal now.
- **Path forward**: A multi-factor checklist (rising_supports + volume_profile + follow_through_absence) could be tested, but would require labeled data.

### NQ-005: Point-and-Figure Counts as Projection
- **Why**: Weis states P&F counts “have no magical powers” and “sometimes fail miserably.” They are used as potential targets, not entry triggers.
- **Retention**: Retain the concept of “cause” or “preparation” measured by congestion width. The P&F count itself is a mechanical approximation of that cause.
- **Path forward**: Congestion width on standard OHLCV bars can be measured as a proxy for “cause” without building a full P&F engine.

## NEXT_ACTION

### Immediate Field Entry (Ready for Field Pool)
1. **Horizontal support/resistance levels** (swing highs/lows, axis lines) — can be computed from daily OHLCV and carried forward as persistent levels.
2. **Trend lines and channels** (normal and reverse) — can be computed from swing pivots and linear fits; flag breaks and confluences.
3. **Spring / Upthrust proxy flags** (FA-001, FA-002) — generate alert fields with probability modifiers (trend context, close position, volume confirmation).
4. **SOT proxy** (FA-003) — compute thrust-length regression on recent swing sequences; flag when slope negative + volume sustained.
5. **Contraction / Apex proxy** (FA-004) — ATR ratio and narrow-range flags; generate alert, not direction.
6. **Effort/Reward ratio** (FA-005) — use volume when available; use true-range proxy for FX pairs without volume.
7. **Sequential bar encoding** (close position, range percentile, gap true range) — encode recent 3-bar sequence as a feature vector for downstream models or human review.

### Extra Data Required (Before Quantification)
1. **Tick or sub-minute volume data** for proper wave-chart construction (Weis Wave) — needs either L1+L2 feeds or 1-minute bar volume with bid/ask classification.
2. **Bid/ask volume delta** to approximate Wyckoff’s “net up/down volume” — requires exchange-level data or broker-provided tick volume with direction.
3. **Point-and-Figure engine** — if P&F counts are desired, a dedicated P&F builder from 1-minute closes is needed. Standard OHLCV does not capture the congestion detail accurately.
4. **Stop-run / liquidity-grab detection** — requires either 1-minute wick data + L2 depth, or DOM snapshots to distinguish a brief spike from a genuine close below support.

### Research-Only Language Assets (Do Not Code as Signals)
1. The “right look” heuristic and the “art of chart reading” boundary warnings — retain in the structured note for all downstream consumers.
2. The “feel of the music” / intuition framing from tape reading — retain for educational context, not as a quantizable field.
3. Absorption vs. distribution judgment calls — retain the checklist; future research may build a multi-factor checklist, but no live signal now.
4. Wyckoff’s “market is like a slowly revolving wheel” metaphor — retained as a conceptual framework for interpreting effort/reward dynamics.
